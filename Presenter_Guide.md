## 1. The talk on one page

**Big idea (say it early, say it last):** ICH is a process, not a moment.

**What the audience should leave with:** a scan of a bleed is not the end of the story. The vessel disease decides where it bleeds. The first hours decide how big it gets. The blood itself keeps injuring the brain for days. Each stage is a window where we can act.

**Story shape:** a hook (the man at dinner), then "what is" (40% die, no progress in 30 years), then "what could be" (three windows to act). Four acts follow, and the talk closes by returning to the man at dinner.

## 3. Delivery playbook

**Opening.** Walk to the centre, pause, look at the room, then start with the story: "Picture a 62-year-old man at dinner…" Do not open with "So…", an apology, or "I'll try to be quick".

**Data slides (6, 11, 14, 15, 19).** Use *point, pause, explain*. Point to the number or bar. Stay silent for two seconds while people read. Then say what it means. On slide 14, let the 100 dots sit for a moment before you speak.

**Pace.** Aim for about 130–140 words a minute. Pause after every key number. Silence feels long to you and normal to the audience.

**Eyes and hands.** Say one sentence to one person, then move to the next person. Gesture towards the slide with an open hand, but keep your body facing the room. Never read with your back to the audience.

**Section bridges.** Learn these word for word. They carry the story.
- Into Act 1: "Let's start with the vessel. Why does a small artery give way?"
- Into Act 2: "We know why the vessel broke. Now let's watch the first hours."
- Into Act 3: "The bleed has stopped growing. The injury has not stopped."
- Into Act 4: "So what does all this mean at the bedside?"

**Slide 21 is the heart of the talk.** Slow down. This is where the audience turns the mechanisms into practice.

**Closing.** Go back to the man at dinner, then say "ICH is a process, not a moment." Stop, and let the silence land before you say "Thank you."

**Nerves.** Before you start, breathe in for 4 counts and out for 6, three times. Hold the clicker in one hand and nothing else. If you lose your place, look at the slide title, say it aloud and carry on.

## 4. Likely questions and short answers

**Why do the deep perforating arteries bleed and not the big ones?**
They branch at right angles straight off large, high-pressure arteries such as the middle cerebral artery. They are small and get little pressure buffering. Years of hypertension cause lipohyalinosis and fibrinoid necrosis, which leave a thick but brittle wall.

**Are Charcot–Bouchard microaneurysms real?**
Charcot and Bouchard described them in 1868. Later work suggests some were artefacts or coiled vessels. Fisher found bleeding points at the edge of the haematoma, which fits his "avalanche" idea. Their exact role is still debated, and the slide says so.

**How do you confirm CAA?**
Definite CAA needs pathology. On Congo red staining, amyloid shows apple-green birefringence under polarised light. In life, we use the Boston criteria on MRI, based on lobar microbleeds and cortical superficial siderosis. Version 2.0 (2022) added white-matter markers.

**Why are anticoagulant-related bleeds so bad?**
The blood cannot clot well, so the haematoma keeps growing for longer. In the SMASH-U cohort, these were the largest bleeds (median 13.4 mL). They also had the highest 3-month mortality, at 54%. That is why urgent reversal matters.

**Isn't lowering blood pressure risky for the tissue around the clot?**
PET showed "hypoperfusion without ischaemia": the tissue uses less oxygen, so it is not starving (Zazulia 2001). In ICH ADAPT (2013), lowering systolic pressure below 150 did not reduce blood flow around the clot. INTERACT2 (2013) found intensive lowering to below 140 was safe. Its ordinal analysis also showed better functional recovery.

**What is the spot sign?**
It is contrast leaking into the haematoma on CT angiography, which means the bleed is still active. It predicts growth (PREDICT study, 2012).

**How is haemorrhagic transformation different from primary ICH?**
In haemorrhagic transformation, the infarct comes first. Ischaemia damages the vessels and the blood–brain barrier, and the tissue then bleeds, often after reperfusion. In primary ICH, a vessel diseased by small-vessel disease ruptures.

**Why do some patients get worse on day 3 to 5?**
Oedema is still building. Red cells break down and release iron. Hydrocephalus can develop. Medical complications such as pneumonia and seizures also play a part.

**Is there a drug for secondary injury yet?**
Not yet. Many drugs worked in animals. The i-DEF trial (2019) tested the iron chelator deferoxamine and did not show a meaningful benefit at 90 days.

**What does this mean for rehabilitation timing?**
Follow the medical team's parameters for blood pressure and activity. Be most watchful in the first 24–72 hours. In AVERT (2015), very early, high-dose mobilisation within 24 hours reduced the chance of a good outcome, and that trial included some ICH patients. Once the patient is stable, short, frequent sessions with a neuro check before each one are the safe pattern. Follow your local protocol.

**Is ICH more common in some settings?**
Yes. Worldwide, ICH makes up about 28% of strokes, and incidence is highest in lower-middle-income countries (Wolsink et al., 2024). Uncontrolled hypertension is the main driver.

**What has changed since the book was published?** Go to backup slide 25. It covers Boston criteria v2.0, INTERACT3, ENRICH and ANNEXA-I.

If you do not know an answer, say: "Good question. I don't want to guess. I'll check and come back to you." That answer builds trust.

## 7. Design system used

- **Colour:** deep ink navy `#13233A` (authority), haemorrhage red `#C1272D` (the accent, used only where it matters) and cool greys. Red always means blood or the key point.
- **Type:** Cambria Bold for titles and Calibri for body text. Titles are 36 pt and body text is 14–16 pt or larger.
- **Motif:** the circle as a haematoma. It grows across the section slides as the bleed grows, and its halo is the oedema.
- **Structure:** every content slide has an action title (its conclusion), one idea, a source line and a footer tracker showing Vessel, Hours, Days or Bedside.
- **Transitions:** Morph on section slides and Fade elsewhere. No flying animations.

`tools/deck-source/` holds the generator scripts, which let the deck be rebuilt exactly if we make changes.
